Australian school enrolments  /  2008–2025

The quiet exit from government schools

Australia's school system has never stopped growing. But in 2020 something changed that the headline number hides.

By Mikhalangelo  ·  October 2026  ·  Australian Curriculum, Assessment and Reporting Authority & Australian Bureau of Statistics

2020 The year government school enrolments peaked, at 2.67 million
−3.0 Percentage points off the government share since then, from 66.0 to 63.2
+18.5% Growth in independent school students over the same five years
83 of 88 Regions where the government share fell. The five that rose are all inner-city
Part one  /  What happened

A twelve-year flat line, then a break

Australian schools run in three sectors. Government schools are run by the states. Catholic and independent schools charge fees and together make up the non-government sector. Government is about three and a half times the size of either of the others, so raw enrolment lines show only the size gap. Setting each sector to 100 in its own 2008 size strips that out and leaves the growth rates.

Highlight one sector to pull it out of the group

Independent schools ended 48% larger than they started. Government schools grew 17% over seventeen years, and all of that growth happened before 2020. Since then the line has bent downwards, which no other sector did.

The system kept growing the whole time. That is what made the turn so easy to miss.

Stacked up, the three sectors look like a steady system: the total climbs every year and gained about 158,000 students between 2020 and 2025. Stacked totals hide what happens underneath, because a band can keep its place while shrinking relative to the whole.

Part two  /  The obvious objection

Is this just fewer children being born?

Australia's birth rate fell through the 2010s, so smaller groups of children are now arriving at school. That should shrink the youngest year levels no matter which sector you look at. If the government sector's decline were only demographics, every sector would be losing its youngest students together.

That is not what the data shows.

Government schools lost 8% of their Year 1 students and gained 12% of their Year 12 students. That pattern is exactly what a shrinking birth cohort moving up through a system looks like. But the other two sectors grew at every single year level, including the youngest ones. The children are fewer, and a smaller share of them are being enrolled in government schools.

The same numbers as a grid. All of the losses sit in one block: the government row, from Foundation through Year 4, plus Years 7 and 8 where families often move schools anyway.

Part three  /  Where

Not a capital city story

A national average can hide a lot. The shift could be concentrated in one state, or in the big cities where families have the most schools to choose between. Zooming in from states, to regions, to individual localities shows something less expected.

Counted in students rather than percentages, New South Wales lost 36,470 government students in five years while its total student population grew. Victoria and Western Australia went the other way. The grey tick shows where each government system would have landed if it had simply grown at the same rate as its state's whole student population.

Press play, or drag the year. Click any region on the map.

Run it from 2008 and the colour drains out of the populated coast after 2020. The deepest falls are in places like Richmond – Tweed on the New South Wales north coast, Mackay in Queensland, the Murray and the Hunter Valley: regional and outer-suburban areas, not inner cities. Of the 88 regions, 83 fell, and the only five that went the other way are all inner-city.

Regions are wildly different sizes, so the map above flatters the empty inland. Splitting the country into equal-sized cells instead shows the real pattern: deep blue almost everywhere a long way from a city, much paler along the populated coast.

The same gradient as numbers. Very remote Australia is 88% government and barely moved, because there is usually nothing else within reach. The sharpest fall was not in the major cities but in inner regional Australia, down 4.6 points in five years.

Show one sector at a time

260 schools opened between 2020 and 2025. Governments built 131 of them, but independent bodies built 104 and they cluster on the growth fringes of Melbourne, Sydney, Brisbane and Perth, and along the same regional coast where the government share fell fastest.

Part four  /  Who

The middle gets thinner

ACARA scores every school community on an index called ICSEA, built from parents' occupations and education and the school's location. The national average is set at 1000. A higher score means a more advantaged community. Sorting schools by that score shows which families moved.

Government schools grew at both ends of the range and lost heavily through the middle, about 91,000 students across the two middle bands. These are ordinary suburbs and regional towns where a Catholic or low-fee independent school is a realistic option and the fees are within reach.

The three sectors sit in different places to begin with. The typical government school scores 984, the typical Catholic school 1046, the typical independent school 1067. Government schools also cover far more of the disadvantaged end, which is the part of the system that is still growing.

Part five  /  One last check

Eight jurisdictions, one turning point

A national average can be dragged around by one large state. Splitting the trend eight ways is the simplest test of whether 2020 was a real break or an artefact of adding New South Wales and Victoria together.

Every panel bends downward after 2020, including the Australian Capital Territory, which had been moving the other way for a decade. The size of the turn varies a lot. The direction does not.

A system can grow and hollow out at the same time.

Where the data comes from

  • ACARA Data Access Program, Australian Curriculum, Assessment and Reporting Authority. School Profile 2008–2025, School Location 2025, and Enrolments by Grade 2008–2025. Covers 11,039 schools, and every enrolment, ICSEA score and set of coordinates on this page comes from these files.
  • Schools, 2025, Australian Bureau of Statistics, released 5 March 2026. Used to cross-check the national and state totals against the official school census.
  • Australian Statistical Geography Standard, Edition 3, Australian Bureau of Statistics. Region and state boundaries, simplified for the web.

All three are published under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0. The Vega-Lite specification for every chart and map on this page, the scripts that build the data, and a list of the source files with their download URLs are in the same repository as this page.

How it was made

Charts and maps are built with Vega-Lite 5. The three maps use an Albers equal-area conic projection centred on Australia, chosen because the question is about how many students live somewhere, so distorting area would distort the answer.

Geography comes from the 2025 school location file and is matched back to earlier years using each school's ACARA identifier, which covers 99.3% of 2020 enrolments. The Other Territories (Christmas Island, the Cocos Islands, Norfolk Island and Jervis Bay) hold well under 0.1% of Australian students and are scattered across two oceans, so they are left off the maps. ICSEA bands use fixed cut points rather than quartiles, so 2020 and 2025 are measured the same way.

Generative AI (Claude) was used to help write and debug the chart specifications and the data build scripts, and to edit the text on this page. All figures were computed directly from the source files listed above.

Words used on this page

Sector
Government, Catholic or independent. The last two together are the non-government sector.
Government share
Government students divided by all students in that place or year, as a percentage.
Percentage point
The gap between two percentages. Moving from 66% to 63% is a fall of three percentage points, not a fall of three percent.
ICSEA
ACARA's Index of Community Socio-Educational Advantage. Built from parents' occupation and education plus the school's location. The national average is set to 1000; higher means more advantaged.
Region
An ABS Statistical Area Level 4. There are 88 covering Australia, each holding roughly 100,000 to 500,000 people. Used here because state borders are too coarse to show where the change is.
Remoteness
The ABS classification of how far a place is from services, running from Major Cities through Inner Regional, Outer Regional and Remote to Very Remote.
Foundation
The first year of school, called Prep, Kindergarten, Reception or Pre-Year 1 depending on the state.

What this page does not show

These numbers describe enrolments, not reasons. They cannot say why families moved, whether fees, class sizes, religion, a new school opening nearby or something else drove the decision. They also stop at 2025, the most recent school census published.
